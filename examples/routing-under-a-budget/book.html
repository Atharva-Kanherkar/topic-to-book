The mechanism

One · What a router decidesThe decision

Chapter oneWhat a router decides

A router exists because the cheapest model that can answer a request is usually not the best model available, and you cannot tell which is which before answering.

The problem, stated as a budget

Every request carries three quantities the operator cares about: what it costs to serve, how long the caller waits, and how good the answer is. A single model fixes all three at once. Routing is the decision to fix them per request instead, which means the router needs an estimate of answer quality before the answer exists.

That impossibility is the whole subject. Every technique below is a way of buying a cheap signal that correlates with quality: the shape of the prompt, its length, a small classifier, a first attempt that a judge accepts or rejects.

Trap

A router is not a load balancer. A load balancer assumes its backends are interchangeable and optimizes for utilization; a router assumes they are not interchangeable and optimizes for a quality-per-unit-cost target. Candidates who describe round robin have answered a different question.

Check yourself
Why can a router not simply always pick the strongest model?

Because cost and latency are part of the objective, not constraints to satisfy afterwards. If the strongest model were free and instant, routing would not exist as a discipline.

The shape of it

The figure below is the one to redraw when asked to describe a router. Four boxes, one dashed return edge, and the emphasis on the estimator — because the estimator is where every interesting failure lives.

Practice sheet oneChapter one

Practice sheet oneWhat a router decides

  1. A router sends 80% of traffic to a small model and escalates rejected answers to a large one. Average cost falls 60%. What has certainly risen?

    c. An escalated request pays for both attempts, so its latency is the sum plus the judge. Answer a is tempting because escalation implies a rejection, but a rejected first answer is not an error delivered to the caller; b moves in the opposite direction.

  2. Which signal is available to the router before any model runs?

    a. Only properties of the input exist before inference. Both b and c describe the output, which is why cascades pay for a first attempt to obtain them — and why interviewers ask you to distinguish a classifier from a cascade.

  3. The estimator itself is a model. What does that add to every request?

    b. Every request pays the estimator, including the ones that would have been cheap anyway. That floor is the reason very small routers are heuristics rather than models.
Out loud, from memory

60 secExplain the difference between a router and a cascade to someone who has built neither.

Model answer

A router decides once, from the input, and commits. A cascade decides twice: it tries cheap, judges the result, and escalates on failure. The router pays the estimator on every request; the cascade pays the whole first attempt on the requests it escalates.

Marked down for: not naming who pays. The cost model is the answer.

What must be in the drawing

Request, estimator, two models, and the return edge. If the return edge is missing you have drawn a classifier; if it is solid you have implied every request escalates.

Two · Follow-up laddersCost first

Chapter twoThe ladder they climb

Nobody asks one question. They ask one, then follow your answer down. Each rung below is an opening that recurs, with what the three grades of answer sound like.

They open with“We are spending too much on inference. Where do you start?”

weak
Proposes a smaller model.
solid
Asks for the request mix first, then proposes routing the head of the distribution.
strong
Asks for the mix, names the quality metric that must not regress, and proposes measuring escalation rate before committing to a policy.

Then“Your router is right 90% of the time. Is that good?”

weak
Says yes, or says it depends without saying on what.
strong
Splits the 10%: down-routing a hard request costs quality, up-routing an easy one costs money, and the two errors are not interchangeable.

Then“The large model starts timing out. What happens?”

strong
Escalations queue behind the outage, so the cascade turns a quality problem into a latency problem. Names the mitigation: cap escalation concurrency and serve the draft answer.

Three · A mock roundForty-five minutes

Chapter threeA mock round, annotated

45:00 read the prompt, close the book,
talk for the clock
them

“We serve 40 million assistant requests a day on one large model. Finance wants the bill halved. Design it.”

you

“Before I design anything: what is the request mix, and what quality metric are you already tracking? Halving the bill is easy if I am allowed to regress quality, so I need to know what I am not allowed to move.”

why Two questions, both of which change the design. Asking them first is what turns the next forty minutes into a conversation.

them

“Assume 70% short lookups, 30% long reasoning. We track a thumbs-down rate of 4%. It must not exceed 5%.”

you

“Then the 70% is the target. I would route on input features first, measure how often the small model's answer is rejected offline, and only build the escalation path once I know that rate — because if it is 30% the cascade costs more than the model it replaced.”

watch The moment candidates go quiet and start drawing. Narrate while you draw: a silent whiteboard reads as a stall.

Appendix A · FlashcardsRecall

Appendix AFlashcards

Click a card to turn it. Mark what you knew; the count and the readiness page remember.

  1. A load balancer assumes backends are interchangeable and optimizes utilization; a router assumes they are not and optimizes quality per unit cost.
  2. It decides twice: a cheap attempt, a judge, then escalation on rejection.
  3. Down-routing a hard request costs quality; up-routing an easy one costs money. Only one of them is visible to the user.
  4. Every request pays it, including those that needed no routing at all.
Appendix B · Final examinationPart one

Appendix BFinal examination

Answer, then grade. Each explanation says why the distractor was tempting.

  1. A cascade escalates 30% of requests. The small model costs a tenth of the large one. What is the approximate saving?

    b. Every request pays the small model (0.1), and 30% also pay the large one (0.3), so 0.4 of the original. Answer a is what you get by forgetting that escalated requests pay twice; c ignores escalation entirely.

  2. Which change makes a routing policy harder to evaluate offline?

    c. Once the decision depends on state the policy itself produced, replaying a logged trace no longer reproduces the decision. a and b are both evaluable on the same logs.
